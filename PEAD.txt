So what is the PID?
PID stands for Post Earnings Announcement Drift.
It is a market inefficiency where stock prices continue to drift in the direction of the
earnings surprise for several weeks after the earnings announcement.
In plain English, if a company beats earnings, the stock tends to keep going up for the following
weeks.
If a company misses earnings, the stock tends to keep going down.
The price reaction doesn't stop at the moment of the announcement, but it bleeds into the
following sessions.
And this might sound obvious, but it shouldn't happen if the markets were truly efficient.
The efficient market hypothesis which has dominated the financial literature says that
all the public information should be reflected into the price immediately.
PID violates this assumption directly.
The earning number is public, the surprise is measurable and yet the price keeps moving
in the same direction for weeks.
That is the definition of a market inefficiency that you can exploit.
And the post earning announcement drift is probably the most studied anomaly in the entire
academic finance literature.
The first paper where the post earnings announcement drift was introduced was in 1968.
The paper was an empirical evaluation of accounting income numbers by Boll and Brown.
They were the first one to document that the stock market doesn't fully react to earnings
on the day where they are announced and their chart of cumulative abnormal returns for good
news versus bad news became the foundational image of the entire literature.
In 1989 Bernard and Thomas came out with the paper post earnings announcement drift, delayed
price response or risk premium, where they formalized the methodology by sorting stocks
into the sales based on earnings surprise, showing that the top minus bottom spread generated
positive abnormal returns in 41 out of 48 quarters, with the average drift lasting for
about 60 days.
And this was the paper that turned PID into an institutional trading strategy.
Livnell and Mendenall in 2006, with comparing the PID for surprises calculated on analysts
